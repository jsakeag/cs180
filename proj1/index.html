Computer Vision Image Alignment

Images of the Russian Empire

Automatically colorizing Sergei Prokudin-Gorskii's century-old glass plate negatives by aligning their red, green, and blue exposures.

Keagan Aromin Project 1 September 2026

Sergei Prokudin-Gorskii photographed the Russian Empire from 1907 to 1915, taking over a thousand photos each with a red, green, and blue filter. The digitized versions we have today are strips of photos with the blue, green, and red photos stacked on top of each other. The issue is that they're in grayscale, and they're slightly misaligned. So for this project, I split each into thirds, applied the color filter, and found the pixel shift that aligns them back into one colored image.

Approach

Alignment metric chosen: NCC (Normalized Cross-Correlation)

We score each candidate shift by cross-correlating the mean-subtracted, L2-normalized channels. The shift with the highest score wins. We choose this over Euclidian Distance (pure L2 norm) because it's more unaffected by the differing brightness of the images, and weighs similarity more heavily.

Approach for larger images: Recursive Pyramid

For the full-resolution .tif scans (∼3200×3700px per channel) I built a recursive image pyramid. I downsampled the images by half using a 2x2 block average, repeating until a small "base case" of at most 50 pixels wide on any dimension. I then aligned at that tiny base resolution, then bubbled upwards while refining the shift with a small +/-2px search to correct rounding error from upsampling. This turned an intractable full-resolution search into a logarithmic number of cheaper, tinier searches, reducing runtime to seconds.

Initial Results

All three small JPEGs aligned cleanly! There wasn't much misalignment, and we knew that because there was no blur between any two color channels.

Across all 17 images in the table above, 16 aligned cleanly and all in a matter of seconds regardless of image size due to our pyramid search. emir is the only image that did not align, most likely because the robes in the image show up across all the blue, green, and red filters with different patterns and leave NCC unable to find a shared structure.
